Fall 2025 Student Alerts Analysis

Harpur College, Binghamton University

Author

Data Analysis Report

Executive Summary

This analysis examines 4583 academic alerts raised for 3250 students at Harpur College during Fall 2025. The central question is whether early warning alerts enable interventions that improve student outcomes.

Key Findings:

  • Students receiving only Low (positive) alerts have significantly better outcomes than those receiving Medium or High alerts
  • Cumulative GPA is the strongest predictor of outcomes - a student with a 3.0 GPA has about a 13% chance of failing, while a student with a 2.0 GPA has about a 35% chance
  • Certain departments (Biology, Chemistry, Math) generate more alerts, particularly warning alerts
  • EOP and SSS students receive proportionally more Medium/High alerts but show similar outcomes when controlling for GPA
  • The data reveal a fundamental challenge: alerts identify struggling students, but cannot tell us whether interventions help

5. Predicting Outcomes: What Factors Matter Most?

We used a statistical model (logistic regression) to understand which factors best predict whether a student will fail or withdraw from a course. Rather than showing technical statistics, we present the results as predicted probabilities - the estimated chance of failing for different types of students.

The Impact of GPA

Comparing Risk Factors

To understand how different factors affect outcomes, we compare predicted fail probabilities for hypothetical students. We start with a typical student (2.5 GPA, Good Standing, received a Medium alert) and then change one factor at a time to see how much it matters.

Understanding the Results (Plain Language)

The model tells us:

What increases risk of failing:

  • Lower GPA is by far the biggest factor. A student with a 2.0 GPA is roughly 3 times more likely to fail compared to a student with a 3.0 GPA (holding other factors constant).

  • High alerts (vs. Low alerts): Students receiving High alerts are about 38.4 times more likely to fail than those with only Low alerts - but this partly reflects that High alerts go to already-struggling students.

  • Poor academic standing: Students on Probation or Dismissal face substantially higher risk, independent of their current GPA.

What the model tells us about special populations:

  • After controlling for GPA, academic standing, and alert level, EOP, Athlete, and SSS status show relatively modest effects. This suggests these students’ outcomes are largely explained by their academic preparation (GPA) rather than their population status.

6. Using Low Alert Students as a Pseudo-Control Group

The Idea

Students receiving Low alerts (compliments like “Keep up the good work!”) weren’t flagged for problems - instructors simply noticed them doing well. These students might approximate what happens “naturally” without warning or intervention. If we assume this, we can:

  1. Build a model of the GPA → outcome relationship using only Low alert students
  2. Apply that model to Medium/High alert students to predict their “expected” outcomes
  3. Compare actual outcomes to expected outcomes

If actual outcomes are better than expected, it might suggest alerts + intervention helped. If actual outcomes are worse than expected, either intervention didn’t help or these students have unmeasured risk factors.

Interpretation

What the analysis shows:

  • Medium alert students have an actual fail rate that is higher than expected by 8.1% percentage points

  • High alert students have an actual fail rate that is higher than expected by 34.5% percentage points

How to interpret this:

If actual fail rates are higher than expected, this suggests that:

  1. Medium/High alert students have unmeasured risk factors (motivation, personal issues, course difficulty) beyond what GPA and academic standing capture, OR
  2. The alerts/interventions weren’t sufficient to close the gap, OR
  3. Both factors are at play

If actual fail rates were lower than expected, it would suggest interventions might be helping students perform better than their characteristics would predict.

Important Caveats

This analysis relies on several assumptions that may not fully hold:

  1. Low alert students as “typical”: Students who received positive alerts may be slightly better than average - instructors noticed something positive about them. This would bias our “expected” rates downward, making the gap appear larger than it truly is.

  2. Course differences: Low alert students might be in different (perhaps easier) courses than students receiving warning alerts for the same GPA. We’re comparing across different contexts.

  3. The compliment effect: Receiving positive feedback might itself improve outcomes for Low alert students, meaning they’re not a pure “no intervention” group.

  4. Timing: We don’t know when alerts were issued relative to when students’ outcomes were determined. Early alerts allow more time for intervention.

  5. Selection into alerts: Instructors who submit alerts may teach differently, or have different student populations, than those who don’t.

Bottom line: This analysis provides suggestive evidence about the gap between actual and expected outcomes, but cannot definitively prove whether alerts and interventions are effective. The true counterfactual - what would have happened to these students without alerts - remains unknown.
